The screen

Everything is
on one screen

The equation across the top, a row of circles underneath, the answer on the right. Nothing else is on screen during a question — no HUD to read, no menu, nothing that needs knowing letters.

Each part of the division is colour-coded, and the same colour paints three things at once: the word, the number in the equation, and the part of the picture it names.

The mechanic

A crane the
child drives

The child does not press a button and watch the game share the cars out. They share the cars out themselves. The left stick drives a crane along a rail above the yard; A over the pile lowers the claw and picks up a car; A over a circle drops it in.

Cars come back out of a circle the same way, so a child who has made a mess can fix it without starting over. Y tips everything back into the pile. There is no timer anywhere — nothing on screen hurries them.

Because placement is free, fair sharing becomes something you achieve rather than something you watch. A sharing question will not accept an answer until every circle holds the same number: if the pile empties on an uneven split, the odd circles turn red, their counts appear, and cars have to be moved between circles until it is level. That moment — realising the shares must be equal before there is an answer to give — is the lesson, and no button-press animation produces it.

A grouping question enforces the opposite rule. A circle holds exactly the divisor and no more; try to drop a fifth car into a circle of four and the claw refuses and the car stays in the grip. A new circle opens only once the open ones are full, so "a group is exactly this big" arrives as a physical constraint rather than a caption.

The fork worth getting right

Two pictures,
same symbols

3 ÷ 3 = 1 is one of two legitimate pictures, and they are worth separating before a line of code is written.

Sharing. Three cars, three circles, one to each until the pile is empty. Every circle ends with one. The answer names cars per circle.

Grouping. Three cars, three to a circle. Fill one and it is already full. The answer names how many circles — the "three cars in one group" reading.

Both are on the curriculum. Most apps only ever animate sharing, which is why a child stalls on "eighteen sweets, three each, how many children?" — that question hands you the group size and asks for the count, and the picture in their head answers the other question.

And here is why the vocabulary matters: between the two pictures, divisor and quotient swap jobs.

Stage 1 — colours only

No words at all. The colour coding quietly establishes which part is which before anything is named.

Stage 2 on — words ride along

The three words sit under the equation permanently, in their colours, in their fixed positions. A teacher-menu toggle turns them off for a class that cannot read yet; the colours stay and keep working.

Now and then — name the part

The game lights one part of the picture and offers the three words on a strip. D-pad to choose, A to confirm — same input as everything else, worth points like any question.

The payoff

At the top of the ladder, when the circles are gone and only 56 ÷ 8 is on screen, "which is the divisor?" is still answerable — it has been in the same place wearing the same colour for six stages.

Input

Switch pad,
Xbox mapping

The pad is a Nintendo Switch layout that Windows hands to the browser as an Xbox pad. That swaps both pairs of face buttons — the positions stay put, the letters printed on them do not.

The trap

The obvious binding for "confirm" is buttons[0], because that is A on an Xbox pad. On this hardware buttons[0] is the button printed B — which is cancel on every Nintendo console the class has ever touched. Confirm must be buttons[1].

Calibration, because Bluetooth lies

A Switch Pro pad reports the standard mapping over USB in Chrome, but over Bluetooth or through a third-party adapter the indices move. Rather than guess, the first run shows one screen — "press the button that says A", then "press the button that says B" — and stores the result. Two presses and the hardware question is closed for good.

Because the pad is the only input, three more things are needed: a "no controller — press any button" screen that polls until one appears, handling for a pad falling asleep mid-lesson, and a keyboard fallback for the day the batteries are flat.

Before the game

Who has
the controller

Thirty students, one pad. The picker draws a number from 1 to 30 — press A, the reel spins and slows and lands, big enough to read from the back of the room. Press A again to start the question.

Names are optional. The number is the identity, which is what makes it work with a class that cannot read yet.

Progression

The picture
has to fade

The point of the pictures is to stop needing them. Every stage runs the same four rungs, and a fluent child spends most turns on the last one — dropping back only when a new divisor appears.

  1. Watch

    The moving runs on its own. The child reads the finished circles and picks the number. Teaches what the picture means.

  2. Move

    The child drives it with A, then answers.

  3. Predict

    The answer is picked first; the moving then runs and either confirms it or contradicts it. This rung is where the learning happens.

  4. Know

    Symbols only. X peeks at the picture and costs points — never time, since nothing in this game is timed.

Feedback

Wrong answers are
shown, not marked

No red cross, no buzzer. If a child picks 12 ÷ 3 = 5, the game moves their answer: five into the first circle, five into the second, and the pile runs dry with the third holding two. Not enough to go round is something you can watch happen.

Too small fails the other way — cars still in the pile when everyone could have had one more.

Which is also the best argument for doing division with objects at all: remainders. One car that cannot join a full group is obvious in a pile and nearly invisible in a symbol.

Two rules about the pictures

Countable
at a glance

Rows, never heaps

Cars inside a circle lay out in rows. Six is two rows of three, not a heap of six. If a child has to count one by one, the picture has stopped teaching division and started teaching careful counting.

Bundle above twenty

Twenty loose objects is about the most anyone can read across a classroom. 60 ÷ 10 uses crates of ten, not sixty cars — which is also the honest on-ramp to place value, so it earns its place rather than being a compromise.

Build

What it takes

One HTML file, 2D canvas, flat high-contrast art, no build step, nothing from the network. Everything is sized for a projector at the back of a classroom rather than a desk.

The moving animation caps at about two and a half seconds however many cars there are — roughly 120 ms each up to a dozen, then it accelerates, and R skips the rest.

Gamepad input is polled per frame rather than evented. The game loop already runs so this costs nothing, but presses need edge detection — this frame's pressed against last frame's — rather than a keydown handler, and every repeatable action needs a repeat delay or one press moves three cars.

Object sets

Cars, apples, coins, ducks, socks, pizza slices — one set per round so the picture stays fresh, all drawn to the same silhouette size so circles stay comparable at a glance. Socks earn their place because a pair makes ÷2 obvious before anything has moved.

Built so far: the crane, sharing and grouping, the fair-share requirement, the colour-coded parts, the random picker, the answer strip and the two-press calibration. Still to come: the four rungs, remainders, crates of ten, and object sets beyond cars.
